# Learning science brief for "Lisa's Island" (Остров Лисы)

Written 2026-11-16 for the redesign (linear path, house, cutscenes, topic packs, more Lisa lines).
Child: Russian-speaking, pre-literate, age 3-6 (exact age unconfirmed). Targets: PT-PT (main), EN, DE.

How to read this file
- Evidence label per finding: **strong** (replicated / meta-analysis), **moderate** (several studies or one solid RCT), **weak** (single small study, other age group, or secondary source only).
- "Design inference" = my own extrapolation from the cited evidence, not a result anyone measured in this exact setting. Treat as a hypothesis to watch in play.
- Almost all word-learning experiments below use 18-36-month-olds or school-age children. Studies in the 3-6 window are rarer; where the age gap matters it is flagged.
- Sources were found via web search in this session. Several were only seen as abstracts or secondary summaries; those are flagged "abstract-level". Nothing is cited that was not found. Numbers marked "unverified" must not be quoted to anyone.

## Executive summary (15 lines)

1. Introduce words in small blocks of 4 (retention of exactly the first 4 new names is the one number the fast-mapping literature gives; max 8 new words per session = 2 blocks).
2. Every new word gets >= 3 clean exposures in the Meet step (picture + the word in a short carrier phrase + an action), with attention drawn to the target (highlight, dim the rest).
3. Retrieval beats re-exposure: after Meet, retrieve each word 3 more times on an expanding within-session schedule (about 0 / 1-2 min / 5+ min), then day +1, +3, +7, +14, +30.
4. Spacing and expanding schedules help generalisation and delayed retention, not immediate scores; do not judge the schedule by the end-of-block score.
5. Interleave old and new words from the second game of a session on; first teach each new word in one fixed context, then vary the pictures (2-3 exemplars) in later retrievals.
6. Keep errorless: answer choices 2-3 (4 only later), immediate corrective feedback (replay word, highlight picture), no failure sound. Helped answers do not promote the word.
7. Recognition (tap 1 of N) is the workhorse; add an unscored "say it with Lisa" echo plus a mimed action, because produced and enacted words are retained better in the small studies we have.
8. Russian stays the language of instructions, emotion and story. Use a Russian gloss only at first exposure, then fade it; evidence on L1 glosses for 3-6-year-olds is mixed.
9. Rewards: make stars and house items fixed, unannounced-as-payment and completion-based (never accuracy-based); build the house as Lisa's story consequence plus occasional unexpected gifts. Expected tangible rewards for an engaging activity are the documented risk.
10. Praise: warmth stays, wording changes. Describe the strategy, effort, the word, or the effect on the world. Avoid "you are smart/the best", inflated praise and controlling "if you... you get..." lines.
11. Lisa's strength is a familiar, consistent character that responds to each touch. Keep every tap contingent and meaningful; remove decorative taps and noisy effects that compete with the word.
12. Cutscenes: 60-90 s, 2 pause-and-answer prompts, the same character, replayable; concepts limited to counting 1-4(10), more/less, add one, day/night as observable facts, sink/float by demonstration, seasons as weather.
13. Number cutscenes can be tied to a linear 1-10 path (number-line board games raised preschool number knowledge and lasted 9 weeks in an RCT).
14. Topic packs: pictures of foods, landmarks, animals, flags and "travel" scenes, not abstract maps; 4-5 words per country, no map-reading test.
15. Screen time: WHO (2019) says no more than 1 hour of sedentary screen time a day for ages 3-4, "less is better". Default hard cap 15 min per session, one session a day, an ending ritual with no "one more", plus a 30-45 s movement break mid-session.

## 1. Exposures, new words per session, session length

**Findings**
- Fast mapping is not the same as retention. 24-month-olds pick the right referent for a new name at once but retain it poorly after 5 minutes; retention appeared only for the first 4 novel names presented when ostensive naming (explicit labelling of the target after selection) was added (Horst & Samuelson 2008). Evidence: moderate (several experiments, but toddlers).
- The mechanism is attention: retention appeared when the target was illuminated and competitors dampened, not when the experimenter only pointed (Axelsson, Churchley & Horst 2012). Evidence: moderate, 24-month-olds.
- Repeating the same context beats varied contexts for retention: 3-year-olds who heard the same story 3 times retained new word-object links, those who heard 3 different stories with the same words did not (Horst, Parsons & Bryan 2011). Evidence: moderate (one study, exposure counts matched).
- Preschoolers can learn and recall a set of items with a few study/retrieval cycles: 3-6-year-olds (mean about 4.8) handled 16 pictures in a 5-cycle design (Kliegl, Abel & Bäuml 2018); 6-7 toy names in Fritz et al. 2007. Evidence: moderate, but these are picture-NAME pairs recalled minutes later, not months of retention.
- Session length / attention span: no peer-reviewed number was retrieved. A common "age plus 1-2 minutes of focus" rule is **unverified**, do not cite it. Evidence: weak/none.

**Numeric recommendation (design inference from the above)**
- Block = 4 new words. Session = at most 2 blocks (8 new words) plus review words. Start at 4 new per day for a 4-year-old, 6-8 for a 5-6-year-old; scale down if retrieval accuracy at +1 day is below about 70 percent (watch, do not trust my threshold).
- Meet step: >= 3 exposures per word within the first minute (hear, hear + act, hear + tap), with the target visually isolated (others dimmed, target lit).
- Active play per session 12-15 min including cutscene (see section 9). Stop early if the child tires; never extend.

## 2. Spacing and retrieval

**Findings**
- Spacing for toddlers: 2-year-olds learning object categories with 30-second gaps between instances did better at a 15-minute delay than massed or simultaneous presentation, while the simultaneous condition was best only on the immediate test (Vlach, Ankowski & Sandhofer 2012; 4 instances per category, each labelled 3 times). Evidence: moderate.
- Spacing for word production: 2-year-olds produced more novel words after exposures distributed over 2 weeks than after massed exposure on one day (Childers & Tomasello 2002). Evidence: moderate.
- Spacing for 5-7-year-olds improved generalisation of science concepts (Vlach & Sandhofer 2012, N=36). Evidence: moderate.
- Expanding vs equal spacing: no difference at immediate test, expanding better at 24 h (Vlach, Sandhofer & Bjork 2014; abstract-level, full method not read). Evidence: weak-moderate.
- Expanding retrieval practice doubled preschoolers' recall of toy names versus other conditions; about half the benefit came from spacing, half from retrieval itself; a reward incentive did not improve learning (Fritz et al. 2007; d = 1.9 vs elaboration, abstract-level). Evidence: moderate, one lab.
- The testing effect in preschoolers is fragile: no effect with free recall; a reliable effect when practice AND test were cued recall; much larger with immediate feedback during practice (Kliegl, Abel & Bäuml 2018; 3-6 years, 28 children per experiment, 16 pictures). Evidence: moderate. Our tap-the-named-picture task is cued, with feedback, so it sits on the good side.
- Optimal gap grows with the retention interval, inverted-U (Cepeda et al. 2008, adults, 1,350 participants, abstract-level: roughly 20-40 percent of a 1-week test delay for short horizons). Evidence: strong for adults, **unverified for children**.
- Interleaving: 3-year-olds retained word mappings better interleaved than blocked in one recent report; 16-month-olds did better blocked; effect depends on category similarity (secondary summaries only). Evidence: weak-moderate. I could not retrieve the exact authors of the 2025 PMC report (PMC12470837), so it is not in the reference list.

**Is the Leitner-box approach right?** Yes in structure: expanding box intervals with at most one promotion per calendar day matches the spacing evidence. Changes recommended:
1. Add an expanding schedule INSIDE the session (the evidence for gains is within minutes: spaced 30 s beat massed at 15 min).
2. After a first-try wrong answer, step down one box (not back to box 1) for words with box >= 3. Design inference: a single lapse in a 4-year-old with a tap interface is noisy. No direct evidence.
3. Keep "helped answers do not promote": consistent with the feedback evidence.

**Concrete schedule (per word)**
| Event | Lag | Format |
|---|---|---|
| Meet (3 exposures) | t0 | picture + audio + action, no choice |
| Retrieval 1 | right after Meet (0-1 other items) | memory or 2-choice tap, with feedback |
| Retrieval 2 | after ~3-4 other items (about 1-2 min) | tap in a mini-game |
| Retrieval 3 | after ~8-10 other items / next game (5+ min) | tap, different picture of the same word |
| Day +1, +3, +7, +14, +30 | calendar | review in warm-up and in games |

Mark as design inference; the 0 / 2 min / 5 min spacing is an application of Fritz 2007's expanding principle, not their exact numbers (not retrieved).

## 3. Word presentation

**Findings**
- Ostensive naming + attention focusing (see section 1): moderate.
- Varied exemplars aid generalisation and speed later vocabulary growth (Perry, Samuelson, Malloy & Schiffer 2010, 18-month-olds). Evidence: moderate for toddlers; **unverified** at 3-6.
- Same-context repetition aids retention (Horst et al. 2011). Resolve the tension by sequencing: fixed picture + fixed phrase for the first 3 exposures, then 2-3 different pictures of the same word in retrievals 2 and 3 and later reviews. Design inference.
- Gesture: 20 French children (mean 5;5) learning 8 English words; reproducing a congruent gesture while repeating the word improved active vocabulary more than pictures alone (Tellier 2008). Evidence: weak (n=20) to moderate in direction.
- Child-directed speech: 21-month-olds learned labels from infant-directed but not adult-directed speech; by 27 months adult-directed speech sufficed (Ma, Golinkoff, Houston & Hirsh-Pasek 2011; secondary description, journal page not read). For 3-6-year-olds the exaggerated-prosody advantage is probably smaller, **unverified**. Our TTS rate (length_scale 1.15) is reasonable; do not slow further.
- Dual coding (picture + audio) as a distinct mechanism for preschoolers: no primary source retrieved, treated as baseline, not a finding. Evidence: not assessed.
- Carrier phrase vs isolated word: no direct study retrieved. Evidence: none. Keep the word isolated and clear at least once, then in the phrase "Onde está o gato?".

**Numeric recommendation**
- Order: word alone (1x) -> word in carrier phrase (1x) -> word + child action (1x). Total 3 audio plays per Meet card, 1 s gap.
- Highlight target, dim others, during every presentation (attention focus).
- Per word: 1 gesture/action that the child can do on the spot (cat = "claws", eat = chew). Prompt in Russian "Покажи, как ...". Design inference from Tellier 2008.
- 2-3 different pictures per word in the content set over time; start every word with ONE picture.
- Keep effects off the word: no sound effect or particle that overlaps the target audio (see Hirsh-Pasek 2015 and Parish-Morris 2013 in section 7).

## 4. Retrieval format: recognition vs production, distractors, difficulty

**Findings**
- Evidence for tap-recognition vs spoken production in 3-6-year-olds: none retrieved. Evidence: weak (gap).
- Cued retrieval + feedback gives a testing effect in 3-6-year-olds; free recall does not (Kliegl et al. 2018). Evidence: moderate. Supports tap-the-cue and against open "name this" tasks without feedback.
- Producing the word while enacting helped in Tellier 2008 (above). Evidence: weak.
- Number of distractors: no study retrieved. **Unverified**. Picture-selection tasks with toddlers use 2-4 choices; our spec (2 -> 3 -> 4 by accuracy) is plausible.
- "Desirable difficulties" for children: nothing retrieved; the closest are the cued vs free recall and expanding spacing findings above. Evidence: weak.

**Recommendation (design inference)**
- Difficulty ladder per word: (1) 2 choices, distractors visually and phonologically unlike the target; (2) 3 choices; (3) 4 choices only after 3 first-try-correct on different days; (4) distractors from the same semantic category last.
- Add an UNSCORED "say it with Lisa" echo after Meet and at the end of a game block (no speech recognition, no feedback, parent can listen). It trains production without making the tap task harder or creating failure.
- Never increase difficulty inside a block after an error; only across days.

## 5. Errors and feedback

**Findings**
- 7-9-year-olds learned novel object names better in an errorless than errorful condition (Warmington, Hitch & Gathercole 2013). Evidence: moderate for school-age; not tested at 3-6 in my search.
- Immediate feedback roughly doubled the testing effect in 3-6-year-olds (Kliegl et al. 2018). Evidence: moderate.
- Not all errorless advantages generalise: a 2024 study of children learning plurals found error-based learning not superior, and sometimes ineffective (secondary summary, Waller, Yurovsky & Nozari 2024, not read in full). Evidence: weak. Not included as a reference.

**Recommendation**
- Keep the SPEC's errorless design: no failure sound, wrong card fades, question replays, correct card glows after 2 wrong taps.
- Feedback timing: immediate (<1 s). Feedback form: replay the TARGET WORD with the correct picture, not only a generic "Да!". Corrective information matters more than evaluation.
- Do not use "no" or "wrong" language; "почти" is fine.
- A correct answer after help counts as exposure, not as retrieval success (matches SPEC).

## 6. Motivation and rewards (stars, house, praise)

**Findings**
- Overjustification, preschoolers: children who already liked drawing and were promised a "Good Player" award later drew less in free play than children given an unexpected award or none (Lepper, Greene & Nisbett 1973; seen via secondary summaries, sample size differs between sources, so I give no N). Evidence: moderate (classic, replicated in later work, but contested).
- Meta-analysis of 128 experiments: engagement-contingent, completion-contingent and performance-contingent rewards undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation, and tangible rewards were more detrimental for children than for college students (Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999). Contested by Cameron and colleagues in the same journal issue. Evidence: strong that the effect exists under those conditions; moderate on generality. I did not retrieve the subgroup figures for VERBAL rewards in children, so claims about praise being safe or harmful in this meta-analysis are **unverified**.
- The conditions matter: the undermining shows for tangible, EXPECTED rewards tied to doing the activity; unexpected rewards did not undermine in Lepper et al. Evidence: moderate.
- Rewards did not even improve learning here: a reward incentive did not significantly improve preschoolers' recall of toy names, retrieval practice did (Fritz et al. 2007). Evidence: weak-moderate (one experiment). So stars should be justified as warmth and story, never as a learning engine.
- Praise review: praise can undermine, enhance or have no effect on children's intrinsic motivation depending on conditions (Henderlong & Lepper 2002; abstract-level only; the usual detailed conditions - sincere, informational, process-focused, non-controlling - I could not verify in this session).
- Person vs process: after a setback, 5-6-year-olds given person praise or criticism ("you are good at this") showed more helpless responses than with process feedback, in two experiments with no no-praise control (Kamins & Dweck 1999); fifth graders praised for intelligence did worse after failure than those praised for effort (Mueller & Dweck 1998, six studies). Evidence: moderate, mostly one research group.
- Generic vs non-generic praise at age 4: "You are a good drawer" led to worse responses to later criticism than "You did a good job drawing" (Cimpian et al. 2007, 24 four-year-olds). Evidence: weak-moderate (small N), but the age matches ours exactly.
- Inflated praise predicted lower later self-esteem in 120 Dutch children and, for those with high self-esteem, higher narcissism; non-inflated praise predicted neither (Brummelman et al. 2017; parents' praise, not a mascot). Evidence: moderate, but different source (parents), so an analogy.

**What this means for stars / house / shop (design inference)**
- Risky pattern: promise a visible tangible reward in advance for doing the activity (stars -> price tag -> shop). That is the documented undermining condition, and the game's core activity (words) is exactly what we want him to enjoy for itself.
- Safer pattern: (a) fixed and deterministic (also required by SPEC), (b) completion-based, never accuracy-based, so errorless play is not punished and nobody has to "perform", (c) delivered as Lisa's story consequence ("we need a table for the word cards") not as a price, (d) a mix of announced milestone items and UNANNOUNCED small gifts at session end, (e) nothing taken away, no price tags, no shop with choosing-by-cost, (f) the star counter doubles as information ("12 words you know") rather than money. Evidence for this exact design: none; it follows from the conditions in the studies above.
- Child choices that are cheap and valuable: where to place the item, which colour. This restores some autonomy lost by the linear path (see section 9).
- Watch signal (from 15.11 files, still right): does he open the game on his own and play after the reward is gone?

**Praise wording**: see section 13 (10 patterns to use, 5 to avoid). The warmth stays; what changes is what Lisa praises (the doing, the strategy, the word) and how often.

## 7. Characters and social contingency

**Findings**
- Video deficit: toddlers (24-30 months, N=36) learned novel verbs from live interaction and from socially contingent video chat, not from pre-recorded video (Roseberry, Hirsh-Pasek & Golinkoff 2014). Evidence: moderate (toddlers; verbs).
- Touchscreen contingency helps only if the touch targets the relevant information: 24-36-month-olds (N=116) learned from video when asked to touch the labelled object's location, not when asked to touch anywhere (Kirkorian, Choi & Pempek 2016). Evidence: moderate. Update vs 15.11: the 15.11 file says "poorly designed interactivity distracts"; the precise result is "touch-anywhere does not help, touch-on-target does".
- Responsiveness on screen alone may not be enough for toddlers: title "Let's chat: On-screen social responsiveness is not sufficient to support toddlers' word learning from video" (Troseth, Strouse, Verdine & Saylor 2018); abstract not retrieved, finding **unverified**.
- Parasocial relationship (emotional bond) with a familiar character predicts better learning from that character, e.g. toddlers learning early math from characters (Gola et al. 2013), and a newly introduced personalised interactive character worked as well as a familiar one in one toddler experiment (Calvert, Richards & Kent 2014); 3-6-year-olds' stronger parasocial bonds with an intelligent virtual character predicted faster, more accurate answers on add-one problems (Calvert et al., Child Development 2017/2018; year differs across sources). Evidence: moderate, mostly one lab (Calvert), all abstract-level.
- What apps need (four pillars): active, engaged, meaningful, socially interactive, with a learning goal (Hirsh-Pasek et al. 2015). Interactive features that compete with the content reduced parent-child dialogue and story comprehension in electronic books (Parish-Morris et al. 2013; 165 parent-child pairs). Evidence: moderate.

**Recommendation**
- Keep Lisa consistent (same voice, same catchphrase, same gentle style) and contingent: every tap on a word item produces the word; Lisa looks, points and waits after a question (visible pause of 2-3 s before she speaks again).
- The strength the child loves (voices, personality, feeling valued) is supported by the parasocial evidence; do not reduce it. Change the content of her lines (section 13), not the amount of warmth.
- Remove or disable decorative taps on word screens; every tap target either carries the word or advances the story. No effect sound over the word audio.

## 8. Using the child's L1 (Russian)

**Findings**
- Storybook vocabulary instruction for Spanish-speaking children learning English with L1 "bridging" is a published design (Lugo-Neris, Jackson & Goldstein 2010); a later citing source says L1 inclusion beat L2-only, but I did not retrieve the results. Evidence: weak (abstract-level).
- Search summary of a Cochrane review (CD014890.pub2; L2 vocabulary interventions for children up to 6): vocabulary interventions may help L2 vocabulary, evidence uncertain. A randomised preschool trial found English-only and bilingual interventions did not differ on the main outcomes; another reported bilingual better; a robot-assisted study found Dutch-only beat Turkish-Dutch. Evidence: weak/mixed; I did not open these primary papers.
- Direct "L1 translation helps or hurts 3-6-year-olds" head-to-head in typically developing children: not found. Verdict: unproven either way.
- From 15.11: Raikes et al. 2019 (home language use in preschool settings linked to better outcomes in both languages) was listed there; I did not re-verify it.

**Recommendation (design inference, keep what 15.11 decided)**
- Russian for instructions, story, emotion, cutscenes (comprehension of the concept is the goal there). Target language only for the target word and its carrier phrase.
- Russian gloss ("кот") only at the first Meet exposure, and then only until the word is in box 2; afterwards the picture + target audio alone (the child should link L2 to the picture, not to Russian). [CHG vs 15.11 where gloss is on by default for every Meet.]
- Do not mix a target word inside a Russian praise line more than once per block (keeps the target sound clean).

## 9. Linear path: session structure with numbers

No study tests a linear vs free-choice path for 3-6-year-olds learning words. What exists: errorless/expanding structures (sections 1-5), spacing gaps with a distractor activity (Vlach et al. 2012 used 30 s of play-dough/puzzles between instances), and the 15.11 SDT argument that autonomy matters (Ryan & Deci 2000, not re-verified by me). So the linear path is a design decision with a known cost: autonomy. Compensate with micro-choices that do not change the learning content (hat, colour, where the new furniture goes, which of two stickers). [CHG vs 15.11: 15.11 recommended picking the world.]

**Session template (one per day; target 12-15 min incl. cutscene; design inference)**
| Min | Step | New / old words | Retrieval role |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00-1:00 | Lisa hello + warm-up, 2-3 due words, 2 choices, errorless | old | day +1/+3/+7 retrieval |
| 1:00-3:30 | Block A, Meet: 4 new words, 3 exposures each | 4 new | encoding |
| 3:30-5:00 | Memory (4 pairs) with the same 4 words | 4 new | R1 |
| 5:00-7:30 | Mini-game X (feed / dress / paint / body / bus / fishing / market, whichever matches the world) with the 4 new + 2-4 due old | 4 new + old | R2, interleaving |
| 7:30-9:00 | Cutscene (60-90 s) + 30-45 s "move with Lisa" action | 0 | gap of ~90 s with a distractor activity |
| 9:00-11:00 | Block B, Meet: 4 new words | 4 new | encoding |
| 11:00-12:30 | Listen & tap (2-3 choices) on B words, with 1-2 A words mixed in | B + A | R1 for B, R3 for A |
| 12:30-15:00 | Game Z: Run with picture gates or a seek-and-find scene using all 8 new + 4-6 due old | all | R3 for B, interleaved |
| 15:00 | Lisa ritual: house item, sleepy fox, "see you tomorrow" | - | ending |

Rules
- New words per block: 4. New words per session: 8 max (a 4-year-old: 4, i.e. Block A only plus review). If more than 12 words are due, run a review-only session (no new block). [NEW; 15.11 said 3-5 new + 6-10 review, consistent.]
- The next block never starts until the previous block's words have been retrieved at least once in a game (no stacking new words).
- Same 4 words appear in memory then in mini-game X then in Z: retrieval 1 -> 2 -> 3 at expanding lags; day +1 warm-up is retrieval 4.
- Memory and listen&tap = recognition with feedback (good); Run gates and seek-and-find = higher load (motor + search); use them only for words already seen at least twice in the session, never for a first retrieval. Design inference; no study on game-type difficulty ordering retrieved.
- Mini-game slotting: feed (food, TPR-style action), dress (clothes), paint (colours), body parts, bus (transport + counting passengers), fishing (animals, sea), market (counting 1-10, prices as numerals only), seek-and-find (scenes; reviewing words with box >= 2, 6 items per round). Slot the one that matches the current world as game X; Game Z rotates through the others as review of older worlds. Every tap target must be word-bearing (Kirkorian et al. 2016).
- Stop rules: hard end at 15 min of play (parent-adjustable down to 10); end earlier when the child gives 3 helped answers in a row (fatigue/ confusion proxy, design inference); never extend for a streak or goal ring.
- No "one more" button after the ritual. If the child still wants to play, the app shows only free-play toys with no new words (no scoring), for at most 3 min, then sleep scene. Design inference; follows the AAP 2026 design advice to nudge disengagement and minimise prolonged-engagement features (press-release wording).

## 10. Educational cutscenes (maths, physics, astronomy, literature, history)

**Findings**
- Pause-and-answer format: experienced Blue's Clues viewers aged 3-5 interacted more overtly with a new episode but did not comprehend more than inexperienced viewers; the interaction habit transferred to another series (Anderson et al. 2000, abstract-level). Evidence: moderate; interaction is real, comprehension gain from interaction itself is NOT shown.
- Repetition: repeating the same episode 5 days in a row increased interaction and comprehension in 3-5-year-olds (Crawley et al. 1999, abstract-level). Evidence: moderate. Supports replayable cutscenes.
- Sesame Street co-productions: positive effects on literacy/numeracy, world knowledge and social reasoning (Mares & Pan 2013, 24 studies, >10,000 children, 15 countries; commissioned by Sesame Workshop). Evidence: moderate-strong, funder bias noted.
- Peg + Cat: home-use randomised trial (about 200 families, 12 weeks, videos + games + app + print) improved ordinal numbers, spatial relationships and 3-D shape identification (EDC/SRI press release only, not the report). Evidence: weak-moderate (secondary source).
- Number board games: Head Start preschoolers who played a 1-10 linear number board game in four sessions of about 15-20 minutes improved counting, number identification and magnitude comparison, with gains still present 9 weeks later; a colour-version control did not (Ramani & Siegler 2008; What Works Clearinghouse "meets standards"). Evidence: strong for number-line games.
- Number concepts: children first map "one" to "four" onto small-set representations; numbers beyond four come about 6 months later (Le Corre & Carey 2007, ages 3-4). Evidence: moderate-strong. Keep exact quantity talk at 1-4; counting 5-10 as a sequence is fine.
- Day/night: even first graders hold intuitive or synthetic mental models (e.g. the sun goes behind hills, clouds cover it); few had the scientific rotation model (Vosniadou & Brewer 1994, grades 1/3/5). Evidence: moderate. A 60 s cartoon about Earth's rotation is unlikely to install it at 4-6; no study of such a cartoon was found.
- Sink/float: preschoolers often predict accurately for common objects but their explanations are inconsistent and performance is poor when heavy objects have low density (Kloos et al. 2010, PDF seen, bibliographic details not verified); 3-5-year-olds were taught to attend to empty space inside objects in Baker et al. 2011 (PDF seen, title not verified). Evidence: weak-moderate.
- Maps and geography: children as young as 3 grasp that a map or scale model stands for a space, but geometric correspondence is hard, e.g. a child said a line could not be a road because it was too narrow for a car (Liben & Downs, chapter and later work, via search summaries). Evidence: weak-moderate.
- History / literature at 3-6: I found no study on learning "long ago vs now" or on folktales as teaching vehicles at this age. Evidence: none retrieved. Mark thin.
- Ideal cutscene length for a standalone segment at 3-6: no study retrieved. 60-90 s is therefore a design choice, not evidence-based (weak).

Dinosaurs and "candle -> lamp" are concrete and safe. Колобок: Lisa is a fox who eats the hero in the folktale; keep Lisa out of that role or pick Теремок/Репка. (Not an evidence point, a character consistency point.)
8. Globe-and-lit-country idea for topic packs: see section 11.

Concrete topic ideas ranked by developmental fit (design inference + above): (1) more/less/equal 1-4; (2) counting on / add one (Calvert et al. add-one); (3) big/medium/small, patterns (ABAB); (4) sink/float by demonstration; (5) day vs night (observable); (6) seasons as weather and clothes; (7) shadows with a lamp; (8) Репка/Теремок with counting; (9) "long ago vs now" picture pairs (candle/lamp); (10) Moon as a shape that changes (older end only).

## 11. Topic packs: geography, culture, history

**Findings**: see section 10 (maps). No study of country-pack learning for 3-6-year-olds found; evidence: weak. What exists suggests young children read concrete pictures (photos, landmarks, foods) far better than abstract maps; they can identify landmarks on aerial photos at about 5 (Blaut et al., secondary mention only, **unverified**).

**Recommendation (design inference)**
- One country = one pack of 4-5 words (a food, an animal or landmark, a building, a word for "hello", the flag) in the same 4-word block format as everything else. No map test, no "where is it" quizzes.
- Replace the abstract map with a journey: Lisa boards a plane, the window shows a landmark photo or illustration, she lands, and you hear the local greeting. The globe may appear for 3 s as decoration, never as content.
- Content stays concrete and sensory: food (hear its name, "eat" it), a sound or tune, a costume for Lisa.
- Order: places he will actually meet (Portugal first, since the move is in early 2027), then Russia/Germany/England.
- Packs are optional and never gate the main path.
- History words (knight, castle, dinosaur, ship): treat as vocabulary themed worlds with pictures; no timelines.

## 12. Screen time

**Findings**
- WHO 2019 (checked on the WHO page): ages 3-4: sedentary screen time no more than 1 hour; less is better; not restrained for more than 1 hour at a time; sleep 10-13 hours. Age 2: no more than 1 hour; less is better. Evidence: official guideline.
- AAP 2016 "Media and Young Minds" (Pediatrics 138(5):e20162591) emphasised co-use and high-quality content, and secondary sources report a 1-hour/day limit for ages 2-5; per the AAP, its February 2026 statement "Digital Ecosystems, Children, and Adolescents" (Pediatrics 157(2):e2025075320) now replaces prior AAP communications-and-media guidance. The press release states no numeric limit for 0-5 and advises designs that "nudge to disengage", autoplay off by default, and minimising designs that prolong engagement. I did not read the statement itself. [CHG vs 15.11: 15.11 cited the AAP 2016 hour limit via Healio; it is superseded, cite WHO for the number.]
- 15.11 (not re-verified): Radesky et al. 2022 on manipulative design in children's apps (doi 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.17641); Hiniker on natural stopping points.

**Recommendation**
- The game is part of, not on top of, the 1-hour daily screen budget: default one session of <= 15 min per day; parent setting 10/15/20; default hard stop, not soft [CHG vs 15.11 SPEC "soft limit by default"].
- Ending ritual: Lisa yawns, the house lights go out, the moon appears, "до завтра!"; no reward dangling for tomorrow, no "you will lose..." message, no daily streak.
- Movement break at about the midpoint (30-45 s, standing: jump like a rabbit, flap like a bird); it also serves the "do it with the body" gesture evidence.
- Parent screen: weekly report and a "do this away from the screen" tip (kept from 15.11).

## 13. Praise lines for Lisa: patterns to use and to avoid

Basis: sections 6 and 7. Warmth and personality stay; the target of the praise changes. All lines are Russian, Lisa uses female forms for herself and masculine for the child. Examples are suggestions for the Opus/copy step, not final copy.

**10 patterns to use**
1. Strategy/attention (process): «Ты внимательно слушал и нашёл! Вот это да!»
2. Word echo (information, the main feedback after most taps): «Да! O gato, кот!»
3. Effort on a hard word: «Слово трудное, а ты пробуешь. Хвост трубой!»
4. Progress against his own past: «Вчера o pão было новым, а сегодня ты его знаешь!»
5. Effect on the world (natural consequence): «Смотри, дверь открылась! Это ты нашёл слово!»
6. Shared success (relatedness): «Одной мне было бы трудно. Мы справились вместе!»
7. Lisa as co-learner: «Ой, а я теперь тоже запомнила o gato. Ты мне помог!»
8. Normalised mistake, errorless: «Почти! Я тоже иногда путаю. Послушаем ещё раз?»
9. Warm, non-evaluative affection: «Фыр-фыр! Мне так весело с тобой играть!»
10. Curiosity/anticipation: «Интересно, какое слово там спряталось?»

**5 patterns to avoid**
1. Trait/person praise: «Ты умный! Ты гений! Ты такой талантливый!» (Mueller & Dweck 1998; Kamins & Dweck 1999.)
2. Superlatives and comparison: «Ты лучше всех! Самый-самый лучший!» (inflated; Brummelman et al. 2017 for parental inflated praise; analogy.)
3. Generic labels: «Ты хороший ученик», «ты всегда всё знаешь» (Cimpian et al. 2007); say what he did instead: «ты хорошо поработал».
4. Controlling or contingent-reward lines: «Ответь правильно, и получишь звезду», «ещё три слова, и мы достроим дом» (expected tangible reward; Lepper et al. 1973; Deci et al. 1999).
5. Over-the-top praise for trivial successes and guilt lines: «Невероятно! Фантастика!» after an easy 2-choice tap, or «Мне грустно, когда ты уходишь» (inflation as above; guilt/pressure belongs to the dark-pattern list in the 15.11 file, Radesky et al. 2022, not re-verified by me).

**Frequency and bank size (design inference)**
- After a correct tap: word echo + a short "Да!" (<= 1.5 s) every time. A personality line (patterns 3, 5-10) only about every 3rd correct answer. Process praise (1, 4) at block end. Big celebration only at block and session end.
- Never the same line twice within 8 lines; at least 8 variants per pattern; target bank 80-100 lines (SPEC currently has lisa_yes1-8). Rotation by shuffle bag, as in SPEC.
- Do not attach an XP number or star to a praise line when the line is about the child's effort or strategy.

## 15.11 rules I would now change (summary)
1. Choose-your-world autonomy (SDT) conflicts with the new linear path; replace with cosmetic micro-choices.
2. XP +5 first-try / +2 helped is performance-contingent; switch to completion-based stars.
3. Russian gloss default ON in every Meet; restrict to first exposure.
4. Wrong answer resets Leitner to box 1; step down one box for mature words.
5. Soft limit default; switch to hard cap default.
6. AAP 2016 hour limit via Healio; superseded by the 2026 AAP statement (no numeric limit in the release); use WHO.
7. "Kirkorian: poorly designed interactivity distracts" -> precise: touch-on-target helps, touch-anywhere does not.
