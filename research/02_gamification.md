# Gamification and motivation design for a pre-literate 4-6 year old

Written 2026-11-15. Source quality is marked: [P] = primary/peer-reviewed or official page found, [S] = secondary summary/press/blog only (claims not checked against the original), [U] = unverified. Many details come from web-search summaries, not full-text reads.

## 1. Motivation theory: what it is, evidence, implication

### 1.1 Self-Determination Theory (SDT)
**What it is.** Ryan and Deci (2000) say motivation and well-being rest on three needs: autonomy (I choose), competence (I can do it and I am getting better), relatedness (someone cares, we share it). Satisfied needs give self-motivation; thwarted needs reduce it [P: source 1, via summary].

**Implication for this game.**
- Autonomy: let him pick which world, which topic, which companion hat. Do not force a fixed path every day. Choice of two or three doors beats one forced door.
- Competence: he must feel "I did that". Tasks sized so he usually succeeds, with visible growth (the companion learns new words with him, a map fills in).
- Relatedness: the companion character, and the parent. Build "show Mama/Papa" moments (a parent screen with a tap-to-hear recap, a printable or on-screen sticker book to show). A game with no relationship element misses one of the three needs.

### 1.2 Overjustification effect and rewards
**What it is.** Lepper, Greene and Nisbett (1973): preschoolers who already liked drawing with markers, and were promised a "Good Player" award, drew less in later free play than children who got an unexpected reward or none [P/S: source 2, only first page and secondary write-ups read]. Deci, Koestner and Ryan (1999), a meta-analysis of 128 experiments, found that tangible, expected rewards tied to doing a task undermine intrinsic motivation; the finding is contested (Cameron and Pierce disagree) [P: source 3].

**Nuance that matters.** The effect is mainly for expected, tangible rewards handed out for merely doing or finishing an activity. Unexpected rewards, informational feedback ("you got 5 of 6!") and rewards tied to real competence are much less harmful [S, consistent across summaries; see source 3 for the full debate].

**Implication.**
- Stars and stickers are not forbidden, but should be (a) informational, showing what he mastered ("you know 12 animal words"), (b) tied to real skill, not to bare time spent, and (c) never promised up front as a bribe ("do 5 tasks to get a sticker!").
- Prefer rewards that are the content itself: a new place to explore, a new friend who speaks Portuguese, a new costume. Unlocking more play is a better reward than a trophy.
- The language task itself must be fun (hear, tap, funny animation). If the only reason to answer is the gate, he will learn "language = toll booth". Gate design: the task is the doorway to a short fun scene, and the same words appear again inside the scene.
- Flag as an open question: no study tests overjustification for second-language apps at age 4-6 [U]. Watch his free play: does he open the game on his own? That is the real test.

### 1.3 Flow and difficulty tuning
**What it is.** Csikszentmihalyi's flow: clear goals, immediate feedback, challenge matched to skill [S: source 4]. Wilson et al. (2019) show in models of learning that training at about 85% accuracy (about 15% errors) is optimal [P: source 5]. Caveat: derived for simple binary tasks and machine learning/perceptual models, not directly for preschool vocabulary; treat 80-85% as a design target, not a proven law.

**Implication.**
- Target about 80-90% success per task, tracked per word/skill (not globally). Drop to 2 choices when he errs twice; go to 3-4 choices after 3 correct in a row.
- Early in a new word, aim nearer 95% (errorless, see 3.4); once known, let it drop toward 80%.
- Mix: about 70% review of known words, about 30% new, so most of a session feels like winning.
- Adapt silently. Never show "level too hard/easy" or a failing score to the child.

### 1.4 Variable vs fixed rewards
**What it is.** Variable-ratio schedules (unpredictable payoff, e.g. loot boxes) are the structure underlying gambling [S: source 6, UK/Loughborough/Newcastle reporting]. Children have trouble tracking value and spending; whether loot boxes cause later gambling is contested [S]. This game has no purchases, but the same mechanism (slot-machine reveals, "mystery egg") makes a child return for the pull, not the content.

**Implication.**
- Use fixed, predictable rewards: "finish 3 words and the door opens", "5 animals in the book = a new friend".
- A small amount of harmless surprise is fine (a funny animation when a word is tapped), but never as the primary reason to return, and never with a "try again to get the rare one" loop.
- No randomized rarity tiers, no "lucky" chests, no gacha.

## 2. Dark patterns and what to avoid

**Evidence.** Radesky et al. 2022 (JAMA Network Open, DOI 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.17641): researchers coded the three most-used apps of children aged 3-5; only about 20% had no manipulative design features. Common ones: parasocial pressure (a character looks sad/begs you to continue or buy), fabricated time pressure, navigation constraints, attractive lures, and ad-based pressure. Lower-education households' children were exposed to more [P: source 7, press summaries of the abstract; exact figures from the journal not verified]. Children this age cannot tell a game screen from an ad or recognise fake countdowns.

**Streaks and loss aversion.** Duolingo-style streaks work through fear of loss; breaking a streak may increase quitting. Evidence here is blog/industry commentary, and a cited JCR paper was not located [S/U: source 8]. For a 4-year-old, guilt mechanics are especially unsuitable.

**Tamagotchi lesson.** The original virtual pet could die from neglect, and owners and parents reported guilt and mourning [S: source 9]. Keep the pet idea, remove the neglect.

**Implication.**
- No streaks, no daily-login bonus that disappears, no "your friend misses you" messages, no sad faces on exit.
- Companion never gets hungry, sick, or sad because he stayed away. It sleeps and is happy to see him. It grows only from things he did, and never shrinks.
- No ads, no external links, no purchases (offline, so easy). Anything for grown-ups (settings, language mix, progress) behind a parental gate (see 4.4).
- No timers, countdowns or "hurry!" anywhere in the child's flow.

## 3. Core loop, sessions, collections, UX for non-readers

### 3.1 Core loop and session shape
Pattern that good kids' apps use: pick a place on a map, play 3-6 small activities, see a clear celebration, a collectible/unlock, a natural end. Hiniker's work on transitions found that natural stopping points make ending screen time easier, autoplay makes it harder, and a two-minute warning backfired in one study [S: source 10].

**Implication.**
- One session = about 5-10 minutes, one "episode" (about 5 tasks plus a short reward scene). After it: a closing animation (companion yawns, waves, goes to bed), a progress-map screen, and then the game rests on a "see you tomorrow" screen. No autoplay into the next episode; the continue button is deliberately one more tap, and the second episode is fine but the third shows the sleep screen.
- Parent-set daily cap (behind parental gate) with a soft ending: the companion says goodnight in the child's voice-over, not a timer bar.
- Build the stopping cue into the story ("the sun is setting"), not a warning.

### 3.2 Progress map, collections, unlockables, companion
- A visible path/map gives competence feedback and autonomy (he picks the next stop). Show nodes as pictures, not numbers.
- Sticker book/collection: one slot per learned word (the word's picture). Empty slot silhouettes create goal clarity without pressure. This is informational reward: the book is a record of what he knows, which he can show to parents (relatedness).
- Companion that grows: it hatches/learns costumes as he masters word groups. Kind rules: never dies, never shrinks, no care chores that punish absence.
- Unlockables should be new content (a new place, new words), not decorative hoarding.

### 3.3 UX for non-readers
- Everything spoken: instructions in his native Russian where needed, target words in the target language, same voice, short sentences. Repeat on tap of a speaker or on idle (after about 5 s of no input).
- Icons and pictures only; no text for the child. Text is only for the parent area.
- Large touch targets: NN/g recommends at least 2 cm x 2 cm for young children (about 4x the adult 1 cm) and notes that tapping is easy for even the youngest, while dragging is hard; offer tap-to-place as an alternative to drag [P: source 11]. Practical: 2.5 cm or more physical size, generous spacing, accept taps that land near, not only on, a target.
- Forgiving input: ignore accidental multi-touch and palms; accept first tap, not tap-and-hold; no swipe requirements.
- Immediate feedback: sound and animation within about 100-200 ms of a tap.
- Hirsh-Pasek et al. (2015): good educational apps are active, engaging, meaningful and socially interactive, and need a clear learning goal; touch alone is not learning [P: source 12]. So make him do something cognitive (choose the picture for a word) rather than just tapping to see animations.

### 3.4 Errors and errorless learning
Errorless procedures (prompt, then fade the prompt) are evidence-based for young children in teaching-skill settings, but the vocabulary evidence is thin and comes mostly from children with developmental disabilities, and "errorless" in practice still has errors in testing [P/S: source 13]. A history of high error rates may harm later learning (same source).

**Implication.** On first exposure to a word, highlight or pulse the correct picture (prompt), then fade the prompt in later rounds. A wrong tap gets a gentle "hm" sound, the correct word is spoken again, and the right answer is highlighted. No buzzer, no red X, no lost points, no lives.

### 3.5 What good kids' apps do (as reported; mostly [S])
- Khan Academy Kids: free, no ads, no subscription or purchases, nonprofit, ages roughly 2-8 [S: source 14].
- Duolingo ABC: no ads or purchases in the store listing, offline play, audio prompts; Common Sense says best in small doses and notes no way to choose where to start [S: source 15].
- Endless Alphabet: animated monsters act out each word, so animation carries meaning (monetization claims conflict across sources) [S: source 16]. Take from it: show the meaning with a picture and motion, not a translation.
- Lingokids: ad-free learner area, but subscription-based [S: source 17].
- Toca Boca / Sago Mini: open-ended play, no levels, no instructions, no time pressure, no artificial incentives, and (for Sago Mini's own materials) no scripted characters [S: source 18; company statements, not independent evaluation]. Take from it: some free play with no scoring, for example a sandbox room with the learned words as toys.

## 4. Platformer and parental gate

### 4.1 Platformer for 4-6 year olds
The only guidance found is anecdotal (indie developers, parent-tested lists, accessibility write-ups), not child-development research [S/U: source 19]. Consistent points: one-button input, no text, frequent checkpoints, instant respawn, no health-depletion or instant-death anxiety. Needs playtesting with real preschoolers.

**Implication.**
- Auto-run (the hero walks forward) with a single big jump button, or tap-to-move. Manual left/right plus jump is likely too much at 4; test first.
- No death, no lives, no timer. A fall puts him back a few steps, instantly, with a funny sound.
- Gates: the gate asks for one task (hear a word, tap the picture, choose among 2-3). Failing never blocks forever: after two misses the correct answer is highlighted (errorless fallback).
- Keep levels to 2-3 minutes so a level equals a natural stopping point.
- Gate tasks come from words he is learning; keep the platformer fun as a toy so the language task is not the only way in.

### 4.2 Parental gate
Apple's Kids Category rules require links out, purchases and other distractions to sit behind a parental gate and bar third-party ads and analytics [S: source 20; check Apple's current guideline text 1.3]. Even for an offline home game, the same principle applies: settings, language choice and progress data live behind a gate that a 4-year-old cannot pass (for example, hold two corners for 3 seconds, or a written number sum he cannot read).

## 5. Do / Don't (12+ each)

### Do
1. Tie rewards to real skill and show them as information ("you know these 12 words"), not as bribes.
2. Make the language task itself fun and play with the words again inside the reward scene.
3. Give choices (which world, which friend, which hat) to serve autonomy.
4. Target about 80-90% success per word, adapting silently; use 95%+ on first exposure.
5. Use errorless first exposure: pulse the right answer, fade prompts over later rounds.
6. Use fixed, predictable rewards ("3 words = door opens").
7. Make sessions 5-10 minutes with a natural ending (companion sleeps, sun sets) and no autoplay.
8. Use a picture-based progress map and a sticker book with one slot per learned word.
9. Build a companion that grows from his actions and is always happy to see him.
10. Voice every instruction, repeat after about 5 s idle, and never rely on text.
11. Use touch targets of at least 2.5 cm, generous spacing, and tap-to-place instead of drag-only.
12. Give instant sound and animation feedback (under about 200 ms) for every tap.
13. Show a gentle "try again" on errors, then play the correct word.
14. Keep a free-play sandbox with no scoring, for open-ended play.
15. Put settings, language and progress behind a parental gate and show parents a clear recap.
16. Make platformer moves forgiving: auto-run or one-button jump, frequent checkpoints, instant respawn.
17. Playtest with the real child and watch for self-initiated return, which is the real success measure.

### Don't
1. Don't promise a reward before an activity in a "do X to get Y" way.
2. Don't use streaks, daily-login bonuses or anything that is lost when he skips a day.
3. Don't make the companion hungry, sad, sick, or dead for his absence; no guilt messages or crying faces on exit.
4. Don't use random loot, mystery eggs, rarity tiers or gacha loops.
5. Don't add timers, countdowns, "hurry" cues or fake urgency.
6. Don't show lives, health bars, death screens, red X marks, buzzers or negative scores.
7. Don't autoplay into the next level or episode.
8. Don't use any ads, external links or purchase prompts, and no tracking.
9. Don't depend on reading anything: no text labels for the child, no numbers as the main way to read progress.
10. Don't require drag, pinch, long-press or precise taps for core tasks.
11. Don't end a session with a warning countdown (the two-minute warning backfired in one study); use a story ending.
12. Don't gate fun behind language tasks so tightly that language becomes a toll; offer fallback after two misses.
13. Don't let the same word be asked the same way every time; vary pictures and context.
14. Don't show difficulty or adaptation to the child; never say "too hard".
15. Don't treat a high play time as success; success is short sessions he asks to repeat.

## 6. Open questions (unverified or needing playtests)
- Exact age-specific touch-target sizes (the 2 cm figure is from NN/g; the 2.5 cm recommendation is a conservative margin, [U]).
- Whether auto-run beats manual control for a 4-year-old; no research found.
- The 85% accuracy rule at age 4-6 for vocabulary: modeled, not tested in this population.
- Whether language gates inside a platformer reduce intrinsic interest in the language (no direct study).
