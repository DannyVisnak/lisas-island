# Synthesis: what works for a 4–6-year-old who cannot read yet

Sources: `01_language_learning.md` (29 sources) and `02_gamification.md` (20 source groups).
Assumed age: 4–6. He speaks Russian, does not read, and starts a Portuguese kindergarten or school in early 2027.

## The short version
At this age a child learns words by **hearing them while seeing what they mean** and by **finding them again
later**. Reading drills and typing do not help him. Motivation lasts when he **feels he can do it** and when he
**chooses** where to go. Bribes, pressure and streaks do not make it last. So the game is built on one action,
"hear a word, touch the picture". It repeats that action across days, wraps it in a small world he wants to
explore, and stops before he gets tired.

## How each finding became a game feature

| Finding (evidence level) | What it means for him | In the game |
|---|---|---|
| Children understand before they speak. A "silent period" is normal (moderate). | Never force speaking and never block him for staying quiet. | Every task is "touch what you hear". Speaking happens with you, off screen. |
| Retrieval beats re-viewing (testing effect). Spaced retrieval helps young children keep words (strong in adults, good in children). | He has to *find* a word again, after a gap, and across several days. | Leitner boxes per word. A word is "learned" (sticker) only after correct answers on 2 different days. The treasure chest brings back due words. |
| Picture + voice together ("dual coding"). Clutter hurts (moderate). | One clear picture and one clear voice. No music over the word. | Large single pictures. The voice plays alone. Sound effects come after the word. |
| Block first, then interleave (moderate). | New words first come in small sets, then get mixed with older ones. | "Meet" (half a world at a time), then "Listen & tap", then Run levels that add up to 2 older due words. |
| Touchscreen learning works when it is **active and contingent**, ideally with an adult (Hirsh-Pasek's four pillars, Kirkorian). | His tap must cause the word. Learning with you counts most. | Every word plays because he tapped. The parent area has a "play together" tip. |
| Bilingual and trilingual children do not get "confused" by languages (solid). Evidence on 3 at once is thin. | Several languages are fine, but one per session. Portuguese first. | You pick a flag per session. Progress is kept per language. German is off until you turn it on. |
| Russian as a bridge helps (moderate, translanguaging). | He should understand *what to do* and *what a word means*. | Russian voice for instructions plus an optional Russian meaning hint after the word ("Russian help", on by default). |
| Short doses: 8–12 min, 3–5 new words plus review. WHO/AAP: about 1 h of screen time a day maximum at 2–5 (screen-time guidance). | Little and often beats long sessions. | Levels are 2–4 min. The default session is 10 min, then a sleeping-fox ending. You can set 10/15/20/30/off and soft or hard. |
| Survival vocabulary for school (design judgement, no study). | He needs "toilet", "it hurts", "I'm hungry" and teacher instructions in the first week. | World 4 "Important words" and world 6 "Kindergarten" (wash your hands, sit down, tidy up, quiet, look, let's paint, outside). |
| European vs Brazilian Portuguese differ a lot in sound and words. | He must hear Portugal Portuguese. | pt_PT voice "tugão"; European words: autocarro, comboio, casa de banho, gelado, "tu" imperatives (Senta-te, Lava as mãos). |
| SDT: autonomy, competence and relatedness drive motivation that lasts (strong). | He chooses, he succeeds, he has a friend. | He picks the world and the fox's hat. Difficulty adapts so he wins most of the time. Lisa the fox reacts to him. |
| Overjustification: rewards promised up front can kill interest (strong, Deci/Ryan, Lepper). | Rewards should *inform* him ("you know these words now"). They should not work as bribes. | Stickers equal learned words. Stars only unlock dress-up items for the fox. No "do X to get Y" prompts. |
| Flow sits around 80–90 % success (modelled, not tested at this age). | Too hard means he quits, too easy means he gets bored. | Choices go 2 → 3 → 4 by recent accuracy and drop back silently. |
| Errorless learning: no failure experience at the start (good). | Wrong answers must never feel like losing. | Wrong card wobbles and fades, the question repeats, and after 2 misses the right card glows. No lives, no death, no X. |
| Dark patterns are common in kids' apps (Radesky 2022, JAMA Netw Open: only ~20 % had none). | Streaks, guilt and loot work, and they are harmful. | No streaks, no daily bonus, no random rewards, no ads, no network. The fox never gets sad. |
| Natural stopping points beat warnings (Hiniker). | Ending a session needs a story, not a countdown. | Each level ends with a celebration and returns to the map. When time is up, the fox goes to sleep. |
| Platformers for this age: one input, forgiving, no death (practice, anecdotal). | Running is the fun part, and the word is the key that opens the door. | Lisa runs by herself and one tap jumps. Gaps bounce her back. 5–6 doors open by finding the right picture. |

## What the research does NOT settle (watch him play)
- Whether auto-run is right for him, or whether he wants to control the speed himself.
- Whether the doors feel like a toll. If he starts skipping the Run levels, the doors are too frequent.
- The TTS voices are good neural voices but not human. In time, record some words yourself, especially German,
  or ask a Portuguese neighbour (a planned v2 feature).
- The real success measure: **he asks to play again**. Play time alone does not show success.

## How you make it work (5 minutes a day off screen)
1. Sit with him for the first sessions. Point, say the word, let him tap.
2. Use the words in real life the same day ("O gato!" when you see a cat; "Lava as mãos!" before dinner).
3. Never quiz him at the table. Praise attempts and do not correct pronunciation.
4. Keep Russian strong at home. It is the base all the other languages build on.
