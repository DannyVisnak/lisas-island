# Research summary: how a 4-6 year old non-reader learns a 2nd/3rd language

Context: Russian-speaking boy, pre-literate, moving to Portugal early 2027. Offline touch game (images + audio) for English, European Portuguese (EP), optionally German.
Method note: sources below were seen in web search results (abstracts/summaries, not all full texts). Claims marked [UNVERIFIED] come from general background knowledge or weak sources and should be checked before anyone relies on them. Evidence strength is my own rating.

## 1. Comprehensible input (Krashen)
What: people acquire language mainly by understanding messages slightly above their level, not by drills or forced speaking.
Evidence: Moderate-to-strong as a general principle (input volume and quality predict child L2 growth). The strict version (output is unnecessary) is contested; a 2025 critique calls the hypothesis conceptually flawed and insufficient on its own, and classroom research (Swain, Pica) argues output also helps. Mostly conceptual debate, little RCT data in preschoolers.
App implication: Make every screen understandable without reading or translation: the picture, the sound and a clear action must make the meaning obvious. Stay at "i+1": mostly known words, one new word at a time. Never require speaking to progress.

## 2. Receptive before productive / the "silent period"
What: many young L2 learners listen and understand for weeks or months before talking.
Evidence: Weak-to-moderate as a universal stage. Roberts (2014) reviewed 12 studies and found the stage poorly defined and methodologically weak. A 2024 scoping review (40 studies) and a 2019 Irish early-years study found silence varies by child; shyness can prolong it; kids who kept using their home language and nonverbal communication did best. Silence is not a sign of delay.
App implication: Primary mode = "hear word, touch the right picture". Optional, never forced, "say it" moments (no microphone grading needed). Do not read a quiet child as failing. Let him answer by touch, point or gesture.

## 3. Total Physical Response (Asher)
What: the child hears a command and responds with movement, no speaking.
Evidence: Positive but thin. A 2021 mixed synthesis reported a large pooled effect (ES 1.13) but with a huge confidence interval (-0.71 to 3.73); young-learner studies are small, often single-group. No preschool-specific meta-analysis found.
App implication: Use action verbs and commands as touch/drag actions ("put the dog in the house", "give the ball to the girl", "open", "jump" with a sound/animation). Fits "active" learning and costs nothing. Pair with real-world parent follow-up ("stand up", "bring me the cup").

## 4. Spaced repetition and retrieval practice
What: re-meeting items after growing gaps, and being asked to recall them rather than just re-seeing them.
Evidence: Strong in general (Rowland 2014 and Adesope 2017 meta-analyses are cited in the child literature; I did not open them). Child-specific: spaced retrieval boosted novel-word learning in typically developing children and those with language disorders; recall after 1 week matched recall at 5 minutes in one pooled analysis. Single exposure to a new word is rarely retained even after 5 minutes; the bottleneck is encoding. 4-6 year olds learn words better with repeated explicit exposure across days; the number of retrievals during training predicts later retention. Small lab samples.
App implication: Per word: introduce, immediately retrieve (touch the right one among 2), again later in the same session, then next day, then 3 days, then a week. Early gaps short (expanding schedules help early success). Store per-word state locally. Count a word as "known" only after correct retrieval on separate days.

## 5. Dual coding (picture + sound)
What: words paired with images are stored in two linked codes.
Evidence: Moderate. Acha (2009), 135 children aged 8-10, found simultaneous words+pictures beat either alone for L2 vocabulary. Caveats: working memory limits in children; redundancy principle evidence mostly adults/L1. For this child there is no printed word, so it is picture + spoken word (+ gesture/animation).
App implication: Every new word = clear single-object picture + native-speaker audio. Avoid cluttered scenes and decorative animation that competes with the target. Add a short meaningful animation of the thing, not random effects. No on-screen text needed.

## 6. Interleaving
What: mixing different categories/words in practice rather than blocking one at a time.
Evidence: Moderate for category learning; some studies show 2-3 year olds retained word mappings better when interleaved. Preschool word-learning evidence is thin. Interleaving feels harder, so parents and kids may prefer blocking.
App implication: Teach a new word in a small block first (3-5 exposures), then mix with old words in review. Interleave similar-looking items only after both are familiar, and only from one theme at a time.

## 7. Minimal pairs and phonological awareness
What: noticing sound contrasts (EP: vowel reduction, nasal vowels, "sh/zh" sounds, "lh/nh") and units like syllables and rhymes.
Evidence: Phonological-awareness training in preschoolers helps (a 2024 meta-analysis of 38 studies, g=0.57 on reading-related outcomes; technology-delivered programs also show positive effects in a 19-study meta-analysis). Rhyme/syllable work is not clearly a prerequisite for phonemes; phoneme-level work links to letters later. Little direct evidence for minimal pairs in child L2 apps [UNVERIFIED beyond general phonetics literature].
App implication: Include "which one did you hear?" tasks for contrasts the child cannot yet hear (EP vowels, English th/short-long vowels like ship/sheep, Portuguese nasal "pão"). Use pictures of the two words. Keep it a game; no abstract sound drills. Good prep for later reading, but do not push letters yet.

## 8. Songs, rhymes, chants
What: rhythm and melody make chunks memorable and give repeated exposure.
Evidence: Mostly positive but low-quality (small, single-site studies, mainly Indonesia/Turkey/Algeria). Suggestive, not proven.
App implication: Short songs with picture-sync (counting, colors, body parts, greetings), repeat-friendly. Use as a break and as a bridge to parent interaction (sing together). Do not rely on songs as the only teaching tool.

## 9. Parent interaction and L1 (Russian) support
What: parents talk, respond, label, and keep the home language strong; translanguaging means using all languages as a resource.
Evidence: Moderate. Raikes et al. (2019): more home-language use in preschool settings linked to better outcomes in both languages for dual language learners. Strong home-language foundations support L2 learning (Vanderbilt IRIS; American Educator 2018). Dedicated translanguaging evidence in preschool: not found in my search.
App implication: Do not ban Russian. Russian can be the instruction/explanation language for the parent menu, and an optional "what does this mean?" Russian audio hint. Keep family reading/chat in Russian, since L1 strength helps later literacy. Provide a parent screen that tells the parent what to say/do offline with the same words today.

## 10. Several languages at once vs one at a time
What: fears that two or three languages "confuse" the child.
Evidence: Moderate to strong against confusion: bilingual infants separate languages from birth and 2-year-olds adapt to the interlocutor's language (Byers-Heinlein & Lew-Williams 2013; Byers-Heinlein et al. 2017). Small processing costs possible. Evidence on 3-4 languages at once for a 5-year-old with only an L1 so far is sparse [UNVERIFIED]. Practical risk is load and time, not confusion.
App implication: Prioritise by need: European Portuguese first (school and daily life), English second, German optional. Keep each language in its own mode/world with a distinct colour, character and voice, one language per session. Do not mix languages within a single lesson (Russian hints excepted).

## 11. Screen time and whether apps teach words
Guidance: WHO (2019): children 3-4 should have no more than 1 hour of sedentary screen time a day, less is better; WHO frames concern around sedentary behaviour. AAP (2016): about 1 hour/day for ages 2-5 of high-quality content, co-viewed where possible (summary-level sources only; primary documents not opened).
Evidence on learning: Hirsh-Pasek et al. (2015, PSPI) four pillars: active ("minds-on"), engaged, meaningful, socially interactive, plus a clear learning goal. Kirkorian, Choi and Pempek (2016): contingent touchscreen interaction helps only when it is genuine; poorly designed interactivity can distract from word encoding. Video deficit: kids learn less from screens than from live people, though interactive designs reduce it. Choice-based "active" learning has not always beaten simple tasks in 2-4 year olds (PLOS One 2020). Most lab studies are short and use specific apps.
App implication: Cap at 10-15 min/day total, hard stop with a friendly end screen. Design for co-play: parent sits with him, game prompts the parent. Meaningful content (things in his new life: kindergarten, shop, family). Interactions that directly serve the word (touch the item named), not tap-for-fireworks. Report to the parent what he learned.

## 12. Session length, words per session
Evidence: No study I found gives a precise "words per session" or attention span for this age. Directional facts: one-shot fast-mapping is shaky; repeated retrieval across days drives retention; background noise harms word learning (one preschool study). Rule of thumb of ~2-3 minutes of focus per year of age is common but I did not verify it [UNVERIFIED].
App implication (design judgement, not proven): 8-12 minutes per session, 3-5 new words + 6-10 review items per session, built from several 1-2 minute mini-games with built-in breaks. Start a new theme only when most words from the previous one pass the day-3 check. Provide a "quiet" mode hint for parents (turn TV off while playing).

## 13. European vs Brazilian Portuguese
Evidence: Language-school and tutoring sites only; no linguistics sources opened. Consensus: mutually intelligible; biggest difference is pronunciation (EP has strong vowel reduction and closed vowels), plus everyday vocabulary (autocarro vs ônibus) and pronouns (tu in EP informal vs você in Brazil). Most learning resources online are Brazilian.
App implication: Record all audio with native European Portuguese speakers (Lisbon/centre-south standard unless the town's accent is known). Use EP words (autocarro, pequeno-almoço, casa de banho, telemóvel, comboio). Warn the parent that YouTube/apps default to Brazilian. Use "tu" forms for children.

## 14. Entering a Portuguese kindergarten/school: survival vocabulary
Evidence: I found no peer-reviewed vocabulary list for Portuguese pre-school; this is design judgement from child-care routines [UNVERIFIED], though home-language support and continued nonverbal communication are supported (section 2, 9).
Priority content (EP), roughly in order:
- Greetings and politeness: olá, bom dia, adeus, obrigado, por favor, desculpa.
- Needs: quero ir à casa de banho, tenho fome/sede, estou cansado, dói-me aqui, não percebo, ajuda.
- Routine words: lavar as mãos, sentar, em fila, lanche/almoço, sesta, mochila, casaco, recreio.
- Teacher commands: senta-te, ouve, anda cá, espera, pára, arruma, vamos.
- Social: o meu nome é..., quero brincar, empresta, é meu, amigo.
- Basics: numbers 1-10, colors, family, body, animals, food, shapes.
- Feelings and safety: triste, zangado, medo, perigo, quente, não.
App implication: Make this the first Portuguese theme. Teach "I need help/toilet/hurt/hungry" first, via pictures he can also point to in real life (consider a printable card for his bag). Role-play scenes ("kindergarten morning") with spoken commands to act out (TPR).

# Top 12 design implications
1. Zero reading: every item = picture + audio + action; meaning is clear without text or translation.
2. Receptive first: main task is "touch the thing you hear"; speaking optional and never gated; silence is fine.
3. Retrieval, not re-viewing: after introducing a word, ask him to find it (2-choice, then 3-4), over and over.
4. Spacing engine: per-word schedule (same session, next day, 3 days, 7 days) stored locally; "known" requires correct answers on different days.
5. Small doses: 8-12 min, 3-5 new words plus review, then stop; total 10-15 min/day, aligned with WHO/AAP caps.
6. Action = meaning: use TPR-style verbs and drag/touch commands; interactions must serve the word, not decorate.
7. Clean dual coding: one clear image + one native voice per word; no clutter, no competing animation or music over the target word.
8. Block then interleave: 3-5 exposures per new word, then mix with older words; separate near-identical sounds until both are solid.
9. Sound contrasts as games: minimal-pair "which did you hear?" for tricky EP and English sounds; rhymes and songs as a warm-up and reward.
10. Russian as bridge: Russian parent menu and optional meaning hint; keep Russian strong at home; one target language per session, never mixed.
11. Built for co-play: parent prompts, a daily "do this offline" tip, and a progress screen; fits the "socially interactive" pillar.
12. European Portuguese first, with school-survival content (needs, routines, teacher commands) recorded by native EP speakers; English second, German optional.
